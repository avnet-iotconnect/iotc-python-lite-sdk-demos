The United States National Park System is managed by the National Park Service, an agency founded in 1916. It protects more than 400 sites, including 63 designated national parks, for their natural beauty, wildlife, and historical importance.

Yellowstone, established in 1872, was the first national park in the United States and is often called the world's first national park. It spans parts of Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho.

Yellowstone sits atop a massive volcanic hotspot and contains about half of the world's active geysers, including Old Faithful, which erupts roughly every 90 minutes.

The Grand Canyon in Arizona is a mile-deep gorge carved by the Colorado River over millions of years. It is about 277 miles long and up to 18 miles wide, exposing nearly two billion years of rock layers.

Yosemite National Park in California is famous for its granite cliffs, including El Capitan and Half Dome, along with giant sequoia trees and waterfalls such as Yosemite Falls, one of the tallest in North America.

Sequoia and Kings Canyon National Parks protect the giant sequoia trees, the largest trees on Earth by volume. The General Sherman Tree in Sequoia is considered the largest single tree in the world.

Death Valley in California is the hottest and driest national park in the United States and the lowest point in North America, at Badwater Basin, 282 feet below sea level. It has recorded some of the highest air temperatures on Earth.

The Great Smoky Mountains National Park, on the border of Tennessee and North Carolina, is the most visited national park in the country, known for its misty forested ridges and rich biodiversity.

Denali National Park in Alaska contains Denali, formerly known as Mount McKinley, the highest mountain peak in North America at about 20,310 feet above sea level.

Zion National Park in Utah is known for its steep red cliffs and narrow slot canyons. The Narrows and Angels Landing are among its most famous and challenging hikes.

Glacier National Park in Montana is named for the glaciers that carved its dramatic mountain landscape. Its glaciers have been shrinking, and the park is a striking place to see the effects of a warming climate.

The Everglades in Florida is a vast subtropical wetland and the only place on Earth where alligators and crocodiles coexist. It protects a slow-moving river of grass and many wading birds.

Acadia National Park in Maine, on the Atlantic coast, features rocky shorelines, Cadillac Mountain, and forests. Cadillac Mountain is one of the first places in the United States to see the sunrise.

The Rocky Mountain National Park in Colorado protects high peaks, alpine tundra, and Trail Ridge Road, one of the highest continuous paved roads in the country.

Arches National Park in Utah contains more than 2,000 natural stone arches, including the iconic Delicate Arch, formed by erosion of sandstone over millions of years.

The National Park Service also manages national monuments, historic sites, battlefields, and seashores, not just the 63 national parks. The Statue of Liberty and many Civil War battlefields are part of the system.

Leave No Trace is a set of outdoor ethics encouraged in national parks: pack out trash, stay on trails, respect wildlife, and leave natural and cultural features undisturbed for others.

Old-growth forests, geysers, canyons, deserts, wetlands, and glaciers show the enormous range of landscapes protected across the national parks, spanning nearly every ecosystem in the country.

A park ranger is a National Park Service employee who protects the park and its visitors, provides education and interpretation, and enforces regulations.

The busiest national parks can require timed-entry reservations during peak season to manage crowds and protect the environment. Entrance fees and an annual America the Beautiful pass help fund the parks.
