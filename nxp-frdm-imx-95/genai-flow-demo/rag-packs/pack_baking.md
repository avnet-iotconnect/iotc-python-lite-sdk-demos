Baking is the process of cooking food by dry heat in an oven, most commonly used for breads, cakes, pastries, cookies, and other desserts. Precise measurement matters more in baking than in most cooking because the chemistry is exact.

Gluten is a network of proteins that forms when wheat flour is mixed with water and worked. It gives bread its chewy structure and stretch; kneading develops gluten, while gentle mixing keeps cakes and pastries tender.

Leavening agents make baked goods rise. Yeast is a living organism that ferments sugars and releases carbon dioxide; baking soda and baking powder are chemical leaveners that release gas quickly when moistened and heated.

Baking soda is pure sodium bicarbonate and needs an acid, such as buttermilk or lemon juice, to activate. Baking powder already contains an acid, so it works with any liquid; double-acting baking powder releases gas twice, when wet and when heated.

Creaming is the technique of beating butter and sugar together until light and fluffy, which traps air and helps cakes and cookies rise and stay tender. It is the first step in many classic recipes.

The Maillard reaction is the browning that occurs when proteins and sugars react under heat, giving crusts, cookies, and roasted foods their deep flavor and golden-brown color. Caramelization is the separate browning of sugar alone.

Chocolate is made from cacao beans. Dark chocolate contains cocoa solids and cocoa butter with little or no milk; milk chocolate adds milk solids; white chocolate contains cocoa butter but no cocoa solids, which is why it is not brown.

Tempering chocolate means carefully heating and cooling it so the cocoa butter forms stable crystals, giving a glossy finish and a crisp snap. Untempered chocolate looks dull and streaky.

A classic sponge cake gets its lift primarily from air beaten into eggs rather than from chemical leaveners. Genoise is a whole-egg sponge, while chiffon cake adds oil for extra moisture.

Puff pastry is made by folding and rolling layers of dough and butter dozens of times, creating hundreds of thin layers that separate and rise dramatically in the oven as the butter's water turns to steam.

Choux pastry is a light dough cooked twice, once on the stove and once in the oven, that puffs into a hollow shell. It is used for cream puffs, eclairs, and profiteroles.

Meringue is made by whipping egg whites with sugar until stiff and glossy. It is the base for pavlova, macarons, and the topping on lemon meringue pie. A pinch of cream of tartar helps stabilize the foam.

Custard is a mixture of eggs, milk or cream, and sugar thickened gently by heat. Overheating causes the eggs to curdle, so custards are often cooked in a water bath to moderate the temperature.

Sourdough bread is leavened by a natural starter of wild yeast and lactic-acid bacteria rather than commercial yeast, which gives it a tangy flavor and chewy crust. A starter is kept alive by regular feeding with flour and water.

Blind baking means pre-baking a pie or tart crust before adding a wet filling, usually weighted down with pie weights or dried beans, so the bottom cooks through and stays crisp.

Butter contributes flavor, tenderness, and flakiness. Its temperature is critical: cold butter makes flaky pastry, room-temperature butter creams well for cakes, and melted butter makes chewier cookies.

An oven should almost always be fully preheated before baking, because chemical leaveners and butter begin working immediately and need the right temperature to set the structure correctly.

Cakes are done when a toothpick inserted in the center comes out clean or with a few moist crumbs, and the cake springs back when lightly pressed. Overbaking dries them out.

Kneading develops gluten in bread dough through stretching and folding, while resting or proofing lets the yeast produce gas and the dough relax and rise before baking.

Common desserts include cakes, pies, tarts, cookies, brownies, custards, mousses, and ice cream. Pastry chefs are specialists who focus on the sweet, baked, and confectionery side of a kitchen.
