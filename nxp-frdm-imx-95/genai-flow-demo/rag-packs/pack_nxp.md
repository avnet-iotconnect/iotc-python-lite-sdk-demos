NXP Semiconductors is a Dutch semiconductor company headquartered in Eindhoven, Netherlands. It is one of the largest semiconductor makers in the world and a leader in automotive chips, secure connectivity, and edge processing.

NXP was founded in 2006 when the electronics company Philips spun off its semiconductor division into an independent business. The name NXP stands for "Next eXPerience."

Although NXP became independent in 2006, its roots reach back much further through Philips, which had manufactured semiconductors for decades. NXP inherited that long engineering heritage from Philips Semiconductors.

NXP became a public company with an initial public offering on the NASDAQ stock exchange in 2010, trading under the ticker symbol NXPI.

In 2015 NXP acquired Freescale Semiconductor for about 12 billion dollars. The merger made NXP a leading supplier of automotive semiconductors and general-purpose microcontrollers.

Freescale Semiconductor, the company NXP acquired in 2015, had itself been spun off from Motorola's semiconductor division in 2004. This is why NXP's product lines trace back to both Philips and Motorola engineering.

The NXP i.MX family of applications processors came from Freescale and joined NXP in the 2015 merger. The i.MX line powers embedded systems, industrial devices, and edge AI products.

In 2016 Qualcomm announced a deal to acquire NXP for roughly 44 billion dollars. The acquisition was ultimately terminated in 2018 after it failed to receive regulatory approval in China.

NXP is a world leader in NFC, or Near Field Communication, the short-range wireless technology used in contactless payments, electronic passports, transit cards, and tap-to-pay systems.

Automotive is one of NXP's largest markets. Its chips are used in vehicle radar, in-car networking, battery management, secure car access, and the processors behind driver-assistance and infotainment systems.

NXP's main product areas include automotive electronics, secure connectivity and NFC, microcontrollers, applications processors such as the i.MX line, and analog and power management chips.

eIQ is NXP's machine-learning software environment, a set of tools and libraries that help developers build and deploy AI models on NXP processors, including model conversion and optimization.

The eIQ Neutron NPU is a neural processing unit that NXP integrates into some of its processors, including the i.MX 95, to accelerate machine-learning workloads efficiently at the edge.

The NXP i.MX 95 is a recent applications processor that combines multiple Arm Cortex-A55 cores, real-time and safety cores, and an integrated eIQ Neutron NPU, aimed at edge AI and industrial applications.

In 2025 NXP announced an agreement to acquire Kinara, a company that makes discrete neural processing units. NXP offers Kinara's Ara-2 NPU as the Ara240 module for higher-performance edge AI.

Kurt Sievers has served as NXP's chief executive officer since 2020, leading the company's focus on automotive, industrial and Internet of Things markets.

NXP operates globally with design centers and manufacturing partners around the world, and it is a member of major stock indices reflecting its scale in the technology industry.

NXP emphasizes secure edge processing, combining computing power with hardware security features so that connected devices can process data and protect it locally.

The FRDM development boards are low-cost evaluation and prototyping boards from NXP that let engineers quickly try out NXP processors, including the FRDM i.MX 95 used for edge AI demonstrations.

NXP's strategy centers on the intelligent edge: putting secure, efficient processing and AI directly into devices such as cars, factories, and smart products rather than relying only on the cloud.
