The Solar System consists of the Sun and everything gravitationally bound to it: eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, and comets. The Sun holds more than 99 percent of the system's mass.

The eight planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. The first four are small rocky worlds; the outer four are giant planets made mostly of gas and ice.

Jupiter is the largest planet in the Solar System, so massive that all the other planets combined would fit inside it. Its Great Red Spot is a storm larger than Earth that has raged for centuries.

Saturn is famous for its bright, wide ring system made of countless particles of ice and rock. All four giant planets have rings, but Saturn's are by far the most prominent.

Earth is the only known planet with life and liquid water on its surface. It sits in the Sun's habitable zone, the range of distances where temperatures allow liquid water to exist.

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and the only other world humans have walked on. It always shows the same face to Earth because its rotation is tidally locked to its orbit.

The Sun is a star, a giant ball of hydrogen and helium that produces energy by nuclear fusion in its core, converting hydrogen into helium and releasing light and heat. It is about 4.6 billion years old.

A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometers. It is a unit of distance, not time, used to measure the vast gaps between stars.

The nearest star to the Sun is Proxima Centauri, about 4.2 light-years away. Even at that close range, current spacecraft would take tens of thousands of years to reach it.

The Milky Way is the galaxy that contains our Solar System, a barred spiral holding hundreds of billions of stars. The Sun sits in one of its outer spiral arms, orbiting the center roughly every 230 million years.

A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. Supermassive black holes, millions to billions of times the Sun's mass, sit at the centers of most galaxies.

Neil Armstrong became the first human to walk on the Moon on July 20, 1969, during NASA's Apollo 11 mission, saying "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind."

The International Space Station is a habitable laboratory orbiting Earth at about 400 kilometers altitude, circling the planet roughly every 90 minutes. It has been continuously occupied since November 2000.

Mars, the red planet, is a cold desert world with the tallest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, and evidence of ancient rivers and lakes. Several robotic rovers have explored its surface.

A comet is a small icy body that grows a glowing coma and long tails of gas and dust when it approaches the Sun and its ices vaporize. Halley's Comet returns to the inner Solar System about every 76 years.

The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, orbits above Earth's atmosphere to capture sharp images of distant galaxies. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, observes in infrared to see the earliest galaxies.

Gravity is the force of attraction between masses. It holds planets in orbit around the Sun and moons around planets, and its strength depends on mass and distance.

Pluto was reclassified from a planet to a dwarf planet in 2006 because it shares its orbital neighborhood with other icy bodies in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune.

A galaxy is a vast collection of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter bound by gravity. The observable universe contains an estimated two trillion galaxies.

The Big Bang is the leading scientific theory for the origin of the universe, describing how it expanded from an extremely hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago and continues to expand today.
