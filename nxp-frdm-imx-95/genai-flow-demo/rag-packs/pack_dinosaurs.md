Dinosaurs were a group of reptiles that dominated land ecosystems for about 165 million years during the Mesozoic Era, which is divided into the Triassic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous periods.

Dinosaurs first appeared during the Triassic period, roughly 230 million years ago. They grew to dominate the land through the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods before most went extinct.

The non-avian dinosaurs went extinct about 66 million years ago at the end of the Cretaceous period, most likely due to a massive asteroid impact combined with volcanic activity and climate change.

Birds are living dinosaurs. Modern birds descended from small feathered theropod dinosaurs, which means dinosaurs never fully went extinct and their lineage continues today.

Tyrannosaurus rex was one of the largest land predators ever, reaching about 12 meters long, with powerful jaws and banana-sized teeth. It lived in North America near the very end of the Cretaceous.

Dinosaurs are generally divided into two groups based on hip structure: the saurischians, or "lizard-hipped," which include the theropods and long-necked sauropods, and the ornithischians, or "bird-hipped," such as Triceratops and Stegosaurus.

Sauropods were the largest animals ever to walk on land, with long necks and tails, small heads, and pillar-like legs. Examples include Brachiosaurus, Diplodocus, and the enormous Argentinosaurus.

Triceratops was a large plant-eating dinosaur with three horns on its face and a broad bony frill protecting its neck. It lived at the end of the Cretaceous alongside Tyrannosaurus rex.

Stegosaurus was a plant-eater with two rows of tall bony plates along its back and a spiked tail used for defense, called a thagomizer. It lived during the late Jurassic period.

Velociraptor was a small, fast, feathered predator about the size of a turkey, not the large reptile shown in some movies. It had a large sickle-shaped claw on each foot.

Theropods were mostly meat-eating dinosaurs that walked on two legs, including Tyrannosaurus, Allosaurus, and Velociraptor. Many were covered in feathers.

Paleontologists are scientists who study fossils, the preserved remains or traces of ancient life, to learn about dinosaurs and other extinct organisms and the history of life on Earth.

A fossil forms when the hard parts of an organism, like bones or teeth, are buried in sediment and slowly replaced by minerals over millions of years, turning them to stone.

Pterosaurs were flying reptiles that lived alongside dinosaurs but were not dinosaurs themselves. They were the first vertebrates to achieve powered flight, with wings formed from skin stretched over an elongated finger.

Plesiosaurs and mosasaurs were large marine reptiles of the Mesozoic seas. Like pterosaurs, they were not dinosaurs, though they lived during the same era.

Many dinosaurs are known to have had feathers, especially the theropods. Feathers likely first evolved for insulation or display before some dinosaurs used them for flight.

The word dinosaur comes from Greek words meaning "terrible lizard," coined by the British scientist Richard Owen in 1842 to describe the giant extinct reptiles being discovered at the time.

Dinosaurs ranged enormously in size, from tiny species smaller than a chicken to sauropods over 30 meters long. Both plant-eaters, called herbivores, and meat-eaters, called carnivores, existed.

The Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction that ended the age of dinosaurs also wiped out about three-quarters of all plant and animal species on Earth, opening the way for mammals to diversify.

Coprolites are fossilized dinosaur droppings, and studying them helps scientists learn what dinosaurs ate. Trace fossils like footprints also reveal how dinosaurs moved and behaved.
